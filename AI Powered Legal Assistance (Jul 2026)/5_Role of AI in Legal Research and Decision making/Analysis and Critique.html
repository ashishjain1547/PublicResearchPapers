Review No. 2026/08 — Legal Tech Literature

The Role of AI Technology for Legal Research and Decision Making

AuthorsMd. Shahin Kabir, Mohammad Nazmul Alam, Jaharna Rafi Chowdhury
PublishedIRJET, Vol. 10, Issue 07, July 2023
MethodNarrative literature review + vendor product catalog (no original study)

Filed as a companion review to the 2019 IJLSI paper on the same subject. Same exhibit format — seven sections, closing with what's usable for a live build.

Exhibit A

Architecture

Not applicable — a catalog, not a system

No system is proposed or built. Section III organizes existing commercial tools into three functional buckets — machine learning for legal research, expert systems/DSS for decision-making, and NLP for document review — and under that umbrella lists ten vendor products for due diligence and contract review (Kira Systems, LEVERTON, eBrevia, JPMorgan COIN, ThoughtRiver, LawGeex, Legal Robot, ROSS Intelligence, Casetext CARA) plus Lex Machina for analytics. That three-bucket taxonomy is the closest thing to an "architecture" in the paper — useful as a category map, not as a design.

Research layerML + predictive analytics (case outcome prediction, precedent retrieval)
Decision layerExpert systems / DSS (rule-based recommendations, risk evaluation)
Document layerNLP + eDiscovery (extraction, summarization, clause flagging)
Useful as a three-layer mental model to scope a product roadmap — not as a technical blueprint. No data flow, model choice, or system boundary is ever specified for any of the ten products named.
Exhibit B

How outdated is the tech stack?

Outdated at time of publication, not just by 2026 standards

This is the more damaging finding for this paper specifically: it was published in July 2023, eight months after ChatGPT's public release and well into the generative-AI wave sweeping legal tech (Harvey, CoCounsel/GPT-4, Casetext's own CARA A.I. relaunch). Yet the paper contains zero references to generative AI, large language models, or transformers — the entire vocabulary is pre-LLM: "machine learning," "NLP," "expert systems," "decision support systems."

Individual product references are stale in the same way as the 2019 paper — in places, almost word-for-word the same:

  • ROSS IntelligenceDescribed in the present tense as actively supporting BakerHostetler across "27 terabytes of data" — the same anecdote circulating since 2017. ROSS Intelligence shut down in January 2021, over two years before this paper's publication date, after losing a copyright suit brought by Thomson Reuters.
  • Katz et al. (2017) & Aletras et al. (2016)The two headline accuracy figures (70.2% for SCOTUS outcome prediction, 79% for ECtHR decisions) are pre-transformer statistical/feature-based ML studies, now six to seven years old at time of citation, and never updated against any LLM-based benchmark.
  • LawGeex claims90% cost reduction / 80% faster review — the paper itself flags that "detailed case studies supporting these claims are not readily available," which is honest, but the claim is still repeated as a headline figure.
  • Legal RobotDescribed as "still in beta" — a 2018-era status carried forward unexamined into a 2023 paper.

Net effect: a paper published squarely in the generative-AI moment reads as if it were frozen in the 2018–2020 "legal AI 1.0" generation (search, classify, extract) with no acknowledgment that the ground had already shifted under it.

Exhibit C

How was the solution tested?

No testing — secondary citation only

There is no original experiment, benchmark, or user study in this paper. Every performance figure — Katz et al.'s 70.2%, Aletras et al.'s 79%, Kira's "up to 90% faster," eBrevia's "50 documents a minute, 10% more accurate than manual review," JPMorgan COIN's "36,000 hours saved," LawGeex's "90% cost reduction" — is lifted directly from vendor marketing pages or prior academic papers, with no independent replication, no methodology check, and in most cases no primary source beyond a company website.

The paper is transparent about this in one place (LawGeex's unverifiable case studies) but does not apply the same scrutiny to the other nine tools listed, which is an inconsistent standard of evidence within the same section.

Exhibit D

What datasets were used?

None — only cited, not disclosed

The authors used no dataset of their own. The two datasets that appear are entirely borrowed from cited third-party studies and are never described in this paper: Katz et al.'s Supreme Court corpus and Aletras et al.'s European Court of Human Rights case corpus. Neither size, time range, feature set, nor train/test split is restated here — a reader has to go to the original 2016/2017 papers to know what was actually tested.

Exhibit E

Plus points of the research

A useful, if dated, product landscape
  • Widest product catalog of the two papers reviewed. Ten named due-diligence/contract-review vendors with specific claimed metrics is more concrete market coverage than most legal-AI surveys of its era.
  • Clean three-layer taxonomy (research / decision-making / document review) that maps reasonably well onto how legal-AI products are still segmented today.
  • Appropriate skepticism in at least one place — explicitly noting LawGeex's efficiency claims lack accessible supporting case studies, rather than repeating vendor numbers uncritically everywhere.
  • Dedicated ethics and liability section (Section V) that names concrete legal questions — who is liable for an AI error, what standards should regulate legal AI — rather than a vague gesture at "responsible use."
  • In-house enterprise example (JPMorgan COIN) is a genuinely useful data point: a large institution build vs. buy decision, with a stated internal ROI (36,000 hours/year), which the vendor-catalog entries lack.
Exhibit F

Gaps in the research

Significant — content and editorial gaps
  • No generative AI / LLM coverage despite a July 2023 publish date — the single largest gap, given how much the field had already moved by then (see Exhibit B).
  • Reference list quality is poor. References [10] and [11] are duplicates (identical Verheij 2020 citation listed twice). Reference [19] is dated "1994" for a paper about IoT that could not plausibly predate the technology it discusses — almost certainly a typo for 2019 or later, left uncorrected.
  • Two reference entries render as literal "[????]" in the published PDF (the Kabir & Alam IoT/AI law-enforcement review, and the Alam/Paul/Chowdhury security-engineering paper) — a formatting error that made it past peer review and typesetting.
  • Citation stuffing / self-citation pattern. Of 26 references, at least a dozen are prior papers by the same author cluster (Alam, Kabir, and co-authors) on unrelated topics — restaurant-review sentiment analysis, IoT forensics, malware detection, golden-visa immigration programs, job-satisfaction studies, civil-litigation delay in Bangladesh. None of these are engaged with substantively in the body text; they appear to pad the reference count rather than support specific claims.
  • Vendor claims reproduced unevenly. Only LawGeex's numbers are flagged as unverified; Kira's "40%/90% faster," eBrevia's "10% more accurate," and COIN's "36,000 hours" are presented with the same confidence but no equivalent caveat.
  • No original contribution. The paper synthesizes existing public information (vendor websites, two older academic studies) without a novel framework, comparison methodology, or evaluation criteria of its own.

Exhibit G

What to take into "Your Legal"

Concrete, transferable ideas

Adopt the three-layer product map

Research (find/predict) → Decision support (recommend/flag risk) → Document review (extract/summarize) is a clean way to scope "Your Legal's" feature set and explain it to buyers who already think in these categories from evaluating Kira-, LawGeex-, or Lex Machina-style tools.

Publish real numbers, and say so when you can't

This paper's own move — flagging that LawGeex's 90%/80% claims lack accessible case studies — is exactly the standard to hold your own product to. Every efficiency claim "Your Legal" makes publicly should link to a real, reproducible case study; if you can't substantiate a number yet, say "early data suggests" rather than a bare percentage.

Report prediction accuracy honestly

Katz et al.'s 70.2% and Aletras et al.'s 79% are respectable but clearly imperfect. If "Your Legal" ships any outcome-prediction feature, show accuracy, confidence intervals, and the case types it was validated on — don't imply certainty a underlying model can't support.

Differentiate explicitly on being LLM-native

Because this 2023 paper (and its 2019 counterpart) never mentions generative AI, it's a useful artifact to show prospects: "legal AI 1.0" was search-and-classify; "Your Legal" is built on retrieval-augmented generation and reasoning over their actual documents. Use this gap as a positioning wedge in sales material.

Consider a build-vs-buy enterprise tier

JPMorgan's in-house COIN system (36,000 hours saved/year) shows large institutions sometimes prefer building internally. An enterprise "Your Legal" tier that offers a customizable, self-hosted or fine-tunable deployment could capture that segment instead of losing it to internal build decisions.

Keep your own citation hygiene tight

If "Your Legal" ever publishes a whitepaper or research page to support its marketing, avoid this paper's pitfalls — duplicate references, unrelated self-citations, unlabeled entries. Buyers and journalists checking your claims will notice, and it undercuts trust exactly where legal buyers are most sensitive to rigor.
