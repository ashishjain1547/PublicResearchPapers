⚖️ Legal Advisor Bot — critique & analysis

JETIR2405853  |  Shyla N, Kalpitha Somayaji, Keerthana VR, Nidhi B, Niharika Joshi

🏛️ Architecture

LangChain‑centric pipeline – the system uses a modular architecture with four core layers:

  • Chat UI – user interface for text (and planned speech/handwriting).
  • Retrieval Service – query handling, input conversion (embeddings), relevance assessment, and database (likely vector store).
  • Assistant API – orchestrates context and communicates with the LLM.
  • LLM (GPT‑3.5 / GPT‑4) – generates responses using retrieved context + prompt.

✅ The architecture is modern (RAG‑style), with clear separation: UI → retrieval → LLM generation. LangChain is used for chaining and deployment (LangServe, LangSmith). However, the paper provides only a high‑level conceptual diagram; no detailed implementation of the retrieval pipeline (embedding model, vector DB, chunking strategy) is disclosed.

🕰️ How outdated is the used / referred technology stack?

📚 Stack components

  • LangChain – cutting‑edge (2023–2024), actively maintained.
  • OpenAI GPT‑3.5 / GPT‑4 – state‑of‑the‑art LLMs.
  • REST API (LangServe) – modern deployment.
  • LangSmith – developer platform for tracing/evaluation – very current.
  • IPC (Indian Penal Code) as knowledge source – relevant.

⏳ Currency assessment

The technology stack is very up‑to‑date (2024). The use of LangChain + GPT‑4 + vector retrieval is exactly what modern legal chatbots use. However, the paper does not specify which embedding model or vector database is used (e.g., FAISS, Pinecone, Chroma). Also, the system is entirely dependent on OpenAI – no mention of open‑source alternatives (Llama, Mistral) or fine‑tuning.

📈 Very modern foundation, but lacks diversity and fails to address cost/offline concerns.

🧪 How was the solution tested?

Minimal empirical testing – the paper shows screenshots (Fig 4–6) of a car lease policy Q&A, but no quantitative metrics:

  • No accuracy, precision, recall, or F1 on a legal test set.
  • No user study or satisfaction survey.
  • No latency, token usage, or cost analysis.
  • No comparison with a baseline (e.g., keyword‑based system).

⚠️ The “Results and Discussions” section discusses qualitative benefits (cost‑efficiency, scalability) but provides no data to back them. The system was tested on a car lease policy (not general legal advice) – which is a narrow domain.

📂 What data sets were used?

The paper mentions:

  • IPC (Indian Penal Code) – but no specific sections or version.
  • “Various documents” – including a “Car Lease Policy (CLP)” for a company (EPAM).
  • No named legal corpus (e.g., Supreme Court judgments, legal FAQs, CaseHOLD).
  • No details on data preprocessing, chunking, or embedding strategy.

❗ The dataset is proprietary and narrow – a single company policy document + IPC. No public benchmark or standard legal dataset is used.

✅ Plus points of the research

💡 Technical strengths

  • Uses LangChain – modern framework for RAG, with built‑in tools (LangServe, LangSmith).
  • LLM‑powered (GPT‑4) – capable of nuanced reasoning and context‑aware responses.
  • Retrieval‑augmented – reduces hallucination by grounding responses in documents.
  • Modular design – UI, retrieval, assistant API, LLM – loosely coupled.
  • Plans for speech & handwriting – shows forward‑thinking accessibility.

🔍 Practical vision

  • Addresses cost, accessibility, and scalability – key pain points in legal services.
  • Human‑centric approach – emphasises user experience and trust.
  • Good literature survey covering human‑centred design, sexual violence chatbot, and legal chatbots.
  • Discussion on future integration with HR systems, sentiment analysis, and personalisation.

❌ Gaps in the research

  • No evaluation metrics – zero quantitative results; only anecdotal screenshots.
  • Narrow domain – tested only on “car lease policy”, not general legal advice.
  • Dataset opacity – no details on the IPC corpus or how it was chunked/embedded.
  • Vendor lock‑in – fully dependent on OpenAI (GPT‑3.5/4); no open‑source fallback.
  • No cost/performance analysis – crucial for a production system.
  • No security / privacy – user queries may contain sensitive legal info; no encryption or anonymisation discussed.
  • No comparison – with keyword‑based, rule‑based, or other LLM‑based baselines.
  • Speech/handwriting – mentioned as future work, but not implemented or tested.

🧠 Ideas to learn and use in “Your Legal” solution

  • Adopt LangChain / RAG – use retrieval‑augmented generation with a vector DB (Pinecone, Weaviate, or pgvector) to ground responses in legal documents.
  • Use state‑of‑the‑art LLMs – GPT‑4 or open‑source alternatives (Mistral, Llama 3) with fine‑tuning on Indian legal corpora.
  • Modular architecture – separate UI, retrieval, and LLM layers for maintainability and scalability.
  • LangServe & LangSmith – leverage for deployment, tracing, and evaluation.
  • Multi‑modal input – the paper’s vision of speech and handwriting recognition is valuable for accessibility; consider implementing with Web Speech API / Tesseract.
  • Human‑centred design – prioritise user trust, transparency, and ease of use.
  • Cost‑efficiency – monitor token usage; consider caching and smaller models for frequent queries.
  • Expand beyond one policy – build a comprehensive legal knowledge base covering IPC, CrPC, Indian Evidence Act, and landmark judgments.

🚀 Your Legal can adopt the LangChain + RAG blueprint while avoiding the paper’s pitfalls: use a diverse legal corpus, implement rigorous evaluation (accuracy, user satisfaction), and plan for offline / open‑source fallback to reduce dependency and cost.

📌 Final verdict: This paper presents a modern, LLM‑based legal chatbot with a sound architectural vision (LangChain + RAG). However, it is critically weak on evaluation, dataset transparency, and domain breadth. It serves as a reference for tech stack (LangChain, GPT‑4) but not as a validated solution. For “Your Legal”, combine the RAG architecture with a broad legal knowledge base, rigorous testing, and user‑centric design to build a trustworthy and scalable system.
