Review No. 2026/07 — Legal Tech Literature

Artificial Intelligence & the Future of the Legal Profession

AuthorsAshmita Mitra & Amulya Baid, Alliance School of Law, Bangalore
PublishedInternational Journal of Legal Science and Innovation, Vol. 1, Issue 2, 2019
MethodPrimary survey (n=60) + secondary literature review

A working critique of the paper's claims, evidence, and technical grounding — filed as seven exhibits, closing with what's transferable to a live product build.

Exhibit A

Architecture

Not applicable — no system was built

This is a literature-and-survey paper, not an engineering paper. There is no proposed system, no pipeline, no data flow, and no described model. The word "architecture" doesn't apply to anything the authors built — only to third-party products they cite, and even then only by name (ROSS Intelligence, LawGeex, CARA.AI, LexisNexis, Kira Systems, Nakhoda), never by mechanism.

What passes for structure is the research design itself: a 10-point framework (overview → hypothesis → methodology → findings → conclusion) borrowed from standard social-science paper templates, plus a country-by-country survey of legal AI adoption in China, Singapore, South Korea, and India. That's a literature structure, not a technical one.

If you're looking for a reference architecture for a legal-AI product, this paper won't give you one — it only tells you which named tools existed in 2018–19 and what task each was pointed at.
Exhibit B

How outdated is the tech stack?

Pre-LLM era — substantially outdated

Every tool the paper names belongs to the pre-transformer, pre-ChatGPT generation of "legal AI," built on keyword search, rules-based NLP, and narrow supervised classifiers — not generative language models:

  • ROSS IntelligenceCited as a flagship NLP research tool. It shut down in January 2021 after Thomson Reuters sued it for scraping Westlaw headnotes — a fact the 2019 paper obviously couldn't know, but a 2026 reader should.
  • LawGeex vs. lawyers studyThe paper's single strongest statistic (94% AI vs 85% human accuracy, 26 seconds vs 96 minutes) comes from a 2018 vendor-commissioned study on contract-clause classification — a narrow, closed-domain task, not general legal reasoning. It predates any large language model.
  • Blue Jay, CARA.AI, Nakhoda, Fa XiaotaoNamed once, with no technical description. Several are effectively dormant or absorbed into other products today.
  • LexisNexis "Lexi Analytics"The product line has since been rebranded and rebuilt around Lexis+ AI (2023), which is generative-model based — the version discussed here is the pre-generative one.

Most importantly, the paper contains zero mentions of large language models, transformers, retrieval-augmented generation, or generative AI — the entire paradigm that now defines legal tech (Harvey, CoCounsel, Lexis+ AI, Spellbook). It was written about a year before GPT-2 popularized the term "generative," and roughly four years before ChatGPT. Treat it as a historical snapshot of legal AI 1.0 (search & classify), not a description of legal AI today (draft, reason, generate).

Exhibit C

How was the solution tested?

No solution to test — opinion survey only

There is no solution, so there is no solution testing. What the authors did run is an opinion questionnaire on 60 people aged 18–25, a mix of law students and non-law students/professionals, asking things like "will AI cause unemployment?" and "should AI automate or augment law?"

This is perception research, not performance evaluation. No accuracy, precision/recall, latency, or benchmark figures were generated by the authors themselves — the only hard performance numbers in the paper (the LawGeex 94%/85% comparison, the ABA "24.5% faster research" claim) are pulled from other organizations' studies and reproduced without independent verification, replication, or methodological scrutiny.

Read the survey results as a 2018–19 sentiment snapshot of a narrow, young, India-based demographic — not as evidence that any AI system works, or as generalizable public opinion.
Exhibit D

What datasets were used?

None — only survey responses

No legal corpus, case-law dataset, contract dataset, or benchmark was used or created. The only "dataset" in the paper is the raw response set from the 60-person questionnaire, and even that isn't published, tabulated in a table, or attached as an appendix — it survives only as pie-chart percentages embedded in the text (several of which render as "[PERCENTAGE]" placeholders where chart labels failed to export, e.g. the automation-vs-risk charts on pages 8 and 10).

Sample size (n=60), sampling method, and demographic breakdown beyond "age 18–25" are not disclosed, so the survey can't be re-run or the numbers audited.

Exhibit E

Plus points of the research

Useful as a framing document
  • Automate-vs-augment framing. The automate/augment distinction (Ch. 2.2) is a genuinely useful lens that has aged well and still organizes most legal-AI product debates today.
  • Cross-country adoption snapshot. The China/Singapore/South Korea/India comparison (Wusong's "Fa Xiaotao," WongPartnership + Luminance, Linklaters + Eigen, Cyril Amarchand + Kira) is a rare, concrete look at who was actually deploying legal AI, where, and for what task, rather than abstract claims.
  • Grounded, named use cases. Contract review, e-discovery pre-filtering, divorce mediation (Wevorce), legal research retrieval — these are real, specific wedges, not vague "AI will transform law" hand-waving.
  • Honest hypothesis testing. The authors state hypotheses upfront and report survey results that partly contradict their own literature review (e.g., 78% of respondents believed AI would substitute lawyers, against the augmentation consensus the literature review had just built) rather than smoothing over the disagreement.
Exhibit F

Gaps in the research

Multiple methodological gaps
  • Tiny, skewed sample. n=60, all aged 18–25 — too small and too young to support conclusions about "will AI cause unemployment," which is fundamentally a question about the views of practicing professionals, not students.
  • Self-contradicting evidence, unreconciled. The paper concludes AI will "augment, not automate" law, yet its own survey shows 55% of respondents expect automation and 78% expect substitution of lawyers — the contradiction is noted but never analytically resolved.
  • Borrowed statistics presented as validated fact. The LawGeex and ABA figures are taken at face value from vendor/industry sources without checking study design, sample, or conflicts of interest (LawGeex is a commercial vendor benchmarking its own product).
  • No raw data, no confidence intervals, no significance testing despite being framed as "quantitative research."
  • Broken/placeholder chart labels in at least two figures (pages 8 and 10 show literal "[PERCENTAGE]" text), suggesting the paper wasn't proofread against its final rendered PDF.
  • No engagement with legal reasoning limits. Nothing on hallucination, explainability, liability for AI error, or data privacy — issues that dominate legal-AI discourse today but were only nascent in 2019.
  • Citation hygiene. Repeated vague "Id at 7," "Id at 11," "Ibid" references make several claims difficult to independently trace back to a source.
Exhibit G

What to take into "Your Legal"

Concrete, transferable ideas

Lead with augmentation, not replacement

Position "Your Legal" explicitly as a pre-filter for human judgment (draft → flag → human sign-off), not a lawyer-replacement. The paper's own data shows users are anxious about full automation — product messaging and UX (e.g. mandatory review steps, visible confidence flags) should lean into that trust gap rather than against it.

Use a time-saved metric as your headline number

The "96 minutes → 26 seconds" and "132–210 hours/year saved" stats are the most persuasive things in the paper precisely because they're concrete and comparative. Instrument "Your Legal" to generate its own equivalent number (hours saved per contract, per research query) from real usage data — and be ready to defend it, since borrowed vendor stats age badly and don't survive scrutiny.

Start narrow: contract review or research retrieval

Every durable use case in the paper (LawGeex, Kira, Luminance, Nakhoda) is a narrow, bounded task — not general legal advice. Pick one wedge task, nail accuracy and explainability there, then expand, rather than shipping a general "AI lawyer."

Build in jurisdiction-awareness from day one

The China/Singapore/Korea/India comparison shows legal AI adoption is highly jurisdiction- and regulation-specific. If "Your Legal" targets more than one region, plan for localized legal corpora, local compliance rules, and separate go-to-market per jurisdiction rather than one global model.

Run your own user research — properly

Don't repeat this paper's mistake of surveying a small, non-representative group and generalizing from it. Before or during beta, survey actual practicing lawyers and paralegals with a large enough, professionally-relevant sample, and publish the methodology alongside the numbers so your own claims hold up better than the ones critiqued here.

Design for the trust gap explicitly

40.7% of the paper's own respondents were "not sure" whether AI predictions on judgments could be relied on. Surface uncertainty in the product itself — confidence scores, source citations back to the actual case/clause, and an explicit "verify before relying" pattern — rather than presenting outputs as settled answers.
