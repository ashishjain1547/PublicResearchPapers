Paper Review · I2CT 2024 · IEEE

LAWBOT, Read Closely

A working critique of "LAWBOT: A Smart User Indian Legal Chatbot using Machine Learning Framework" — architecture, tech-stack currency, evaluation rigor, and what's actually worth borrowing.

Authors

Nikita, Srivastav, Patel, Singh, Sharma, Rana, Mehta — SVNIT Surat

Venue

IEEE I2CT, Pune, Apr 2024

Core stack

RASA · Longformer · Cosine Similarity · Botpress

01

Architecture

LAWBOT is not one system but three independent lookup tools glued behind a shared menu. There's no unifying model or shared representation across them — each functionality has its own database, its own matching logic, and its own code path.

ModuleInputMatching methodOutput
Case StudyCourt, case type, free keywordsLongformer embeddings + cosine similarityTop 3 judgment links
Lawyer InformationCourt, state, case typePython pattern/string matchingLawyer name, contact, practice area
Legal FAQsFree-text questionRASA intent classifier (sklearn), 20 intents × 5 examplesCanned answer for matched intent

The flow is menu-driven rather than conversational: the user picks court → case type → keywords through button-style prompts (Fig. 5–7), and only the FAQ module accepts open text. Data collection (judgments, lawyer listings) is a one-time offline scrape; there is no pipeline shown for refreshing these databases, so the "database" is really a static snapshot baked in at build time.

Read this as

A prototype orchestration layer (RASA + Botpress front end) sitting on top of three disconnected retrieval scripts, not a single trained "legal understanding" system.

02

How outdated is the tech stack?

Dated by 2026 standards Reasonable for 2023–24 academic scope

Judged against what's available today, this is a pre-LLM-era stack, and several choices were already conservative even at publication time.

  • Longformer for embeddings (2020 model) — chosen for long-context handling, but it predates instruction-tuned and legal-domain-specific embedding models. No comparison against Legal-BERT, InLegalBERT, or modern sentence-embedding models (e.g. E5, BGE) is offered, despite Legal-BERT appearing in their own literature survey.
  • RASA + sklearn intent classifier — solid, production-grade for narrow-domain slot filling, but a 20-intent classifier with 5 examples each is a toy-sized training set; it cannot generalize to real query phrasing variance.
  • No generative model anywhere in the pipeline — every answer is a retrieved document link, a database record, or a canned FAQ string. There's no synthesis, summarization, or explanation layer — the exact gap that instruction-tuned LLMs (open or API-based) now close cheaply.
  • Cosine similarity over raw embeddings — no re-ranking, no hybrid sparse+dense retrieval (BM25 + embeddings), which the paper's own cited work (Khazaeli et al.) shows outperforms embeddings alone.
  • Static, scraped databases — no vector database, no incremental indexing, no mention of how new judgments or lawyer listings would be ingested over time.

None of this makes the paper worthless — it documents a clean, explainable, low-compute pipeline. But if you're building today, treat every component here as the 2023 baseline to beat, not the target architecture.

03

How was the solution tested?

No formal evaluation

This is the paper's weakest point. There is no quantitative evaluation anywhere in the text — no accuracy, precision/recall, F1, intent-classification accuracy, or user study numbers of any kind.

  • "Testing" consists entirely of screenshots of the chatbot in use (Fig. 7–10): a lawyer search, a case search returning three links, and an FAQ answer for "how to file a FIR."
  • The cosine similarity table (Table III) is illustrative sample data for 3 keywords against 5 documents — it demonstrates the mechanism, not a benchmark result.
  • There's no held-out test set, no comparison baseline, no measure of retrieval relevance (e.g. precision@3 for the case-matching function), and no legal-expert validation of FAQ answers or retrieved cases.
  • No latency, scalability, or failure-mode discussion (e.g. what happens with zero keyword matches, or an out-of-scope legal question).

Implication

Every functional claim in the paper ("provides the most relevant lawyer information," "most similar cases") is asserted from a working demo, not measured. Treat the system as a functioning prototype with unverified accuracy.

05

Plus points of the research

  • India-specific framing. Most cited prior work targets Thai, Indonesian, or generic Western legal QA — this paper explicitly fills the "Indian legal chatbot" gap the authors identify in their own literature review.
  • Practical, buildable scope. Three well-bounded functions (case search, lawyer search, FAQ) rather than an ambitious general legal-reasoning claim — a realistic scope for a student/academic project.
  • Legal stopword construction is a genuinely useful, transferable idea. Removing terms like "court," "judge," "petitioner" before embedding prevents every legal document from looking similar due to shared boilerplate vocabulary — a real, practical fix for legal-domain similarity search.
  • Multi-service integration (Telegram deployment) shows the system was taken past a notebook demo into an actual messaging interface.
  • Clear architecture diagrams and workflow figures make the pipeline easy to follow and reproduce at a high level, even without published code.
  • Honest literature survey that correctly identifies its own related work's stronger techniques (BM25 + Legal-BERT hybrid, SVM-Rank, CNN-based QA) rather than overstating novelty.
06

Gaps in the research

  • No evaluation metrics — covered in point 3, but worth restating as the single biggest gap: there's no way to know if this system actually works better than keyword search.
  • No legal accuracy or liability discussion. A legal-advice-adjacent tool with zero mention of disclaimers, hallucination risk, or the difference between "information" and "advice" is a real gap for anything India-deployed, given Bar Council and unauthorized-practice-of-law concerns.
  • Static data, no freshness strategy. Judgments and lawyer listings are scraped once; there's no update mechanism, so the system degrades the moment new case law or lawyer registrations change.
  • No multilingual support despite this being explicitly flagged by the authors as future work — a significant gap for an "Indian" legal chatbot given the country's linguistic diversity.
  • Shallow FAQ coverage. 20 intents with 5 examples each cannot cover the breadth of common legal questions; no discussion of fallback behavior when a query doesn't match any intent.
  • No user study. No usability testing, no target-user (rural/urban, literate/semi-literate) validation, despite "average citizen" being the stated audience.
  • Case retrieval quality is unverified. Nothing confirms the top-3 documents returned for a query are actually the most legally relevant ones — only that they have the highest cosine similarity, which is a proxy, not ground truth.
  • Lawyer matching is pure pattern matching, with no ranking by qualification, rating, availability, or verification of the lawyer's current registration status.
07

What to take into "Your Legal"

Some of this paper's structure is worth reusing directly; some of its choices are exactly what a modern build should replace.

Borrow

Legal stopword removal before embedding

Build (or find) an Indian legal stopword list — court/procedural boilerplate terms — and strip it before any similarity search. Cheap, and directly improves retrieval signal-to-noise.

Borrow

Three-function decomposition

Splitting "find case," "find lawyer," "answer FAQ" into separate, independently testable retrieval problems is a sound product structure — keep the separation, even if you unify the backend later.

Replace

Longformer + raw cosine similarity

Swap for a modern legal or general-purpose embedding model with a proper vector DB, plus hybrid BM25+dense retrieval and a re-ranking step — this alone should beat their case-search accuracy significantly.

Replace

20-intent RASA FAQ classifier

Replace hand-authored intents with retrieval-augmented generation over a real legal-FAQ/statute corpus, so answers aren't capped at whatever 20 topics someone thought to write examples for.

Borrow

Menu-guided narrowing before free text

Court → case-type → keyword funneling reduces ambiguity before search runs. Worth keeping as an optional "guided mode" alongside free-text chat, especially for less tech-literate users.

Fix from day one

No evaluation harness

Don't repeat their biggest gap — build a labeled eval set (queries → known-good judgments/lawyers/answers) early and track precision@k, so you have actual evidence the system works, not just demo screenshots.

Add

Data freshness pipeline

Instead of a one-time scrape, design an incremental ingestion job (new Indian Kanoon judgments, Bar Council lawyer records) so the knowledge base doesn't go stale the day after launch.

Add

Disclaimers & scope boundaries

Bake in explicit "this is legal information, not legal advice" framing and refusal behavior for out-of-scope requests — something entirely absent here and a real liability gap for an India-facing product.

One-line takeaway

LAWBOT is most useful to you as a scoped feature map (case search / lawyer search / FAQ) and a reminder to preprocess legal text with domain-aware stopwords — not as a reference architecture, since its retrieval, evaluation, and generation layers are all behind what's cheaply achievable now.
